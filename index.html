Automating bone marrow cell counts, then finding out why the first version was wrong

An end-to-end computational pathology pipeline, plus the validation framework that compared it to real pathologists and exposed a sampling bug worth fixing.

The problem

Diagnosing leukemias and other blood cancers starts with a differential: a pathologist looks at a bone marrow aspirate slide and manually counts and classifies several hundred cells by type. It is slow, tiring, and varies between people. Automating the count is tempting. Automating it in a way a pathologist should actually trust is the real problem.

What I built

A pipeline that takes a whole-slide image and produces a differential count, with a review interface so pathologists can correct it. Their corrections are stored as new training labels, so the system gets better where it is weakest.

Why a foundation model with a small head on top

Labelled bone marrow cells are scarce and expensive: every label is a pathologist's time. So instead of training a classifier from pixels, each cell is passed through DinoBloom, a vision model pre-trained on millions of blood and marrow cells, which turns it into a vector where similar cells land near each other. A small classifier head trained on the public BMC dataset then only has to draw boundaries in a space that already makes sense.

The part I'm proudest of: the validation

Most ML projects report accuracy on a benchmark and stop. Instead I compared the pipeline's differential counts against real pathologist differentials from the same slides. The first result was bad: the correlation was negative. Rather than tune hyperparameters, I treated it as a diagnosis problem and worked through the hypotheses in order.

  1. Segmentation or crop bugsChecked crops by eye and by size distribution. Cells were intact.
    Ruled out
  2. Red blood cell contaminationRBCs were being filtered before classification.
    Ruled out
  3. Stain normalizationNormalizing stains changed little.
    Ruled out
  4. Confusing maturation stagesSome, but not enough to flip the sign.
    Partial
  5. Counting the wrong region of the slideThe pipeline sampled the dense spicule core. Pathologists count the thin trail of cells spread behind it. Different cells, different proportions.
    Root cause

I designed image-appearance filters so the pipeline samples the trail the way a pathologist's eye does. That one change moved agreement from negative to strongly positive.

What's next, and why it's within reach

One failure mode is left: the classifier was trained on a public dataset, and some of the lab's plasma cells come out looking like basophils to it. That is a textbook domain shift, and the pipeline was built to close it. The review interface shows a pathologist each prediction, takes a one-click correction, and stores it as an in-house label. A few hundred of those is enough to fine-tune the head on the lab's own slides.

My summer research scholarship ended before that review round could happen, so I handed off the pipeline with the failure documented, the evidence attached, and the UI ready. Once the lab has a pathologist's time to spend on the review, the fix is a fine-tuning run away, and the same loop keeps improving the model after that.

Hyde Park Violation Checker: what the landlord said vs. what the city recorded

Paste a building address and see the City of Chicago's violation history before you sign the lease.

Why I built it

I signed a lease on a sixth-floor apartment after the landlord told me the elevator would be repaired by move-in. It wasn't. What stung was learning afterward that the city publishes every building inspection and violation, and that a one-minute search would have shown me the open elevator violations before I toured. Most people I know in Hyde Park have a version of this story. It costs students time and money at exactly the moment they can least afford either.

So I built the tool I wish I'd had, for myself and for other UChicago students.

The design problem

The window where this information is useful is tiny: between getting an address from a Facebook post or a DM and deciding whether to tour. Nobody opens a city data portal in that window. The tool had to be instant, forgiving of messy addresses, and honest about what the records do and don't mean.

The judgment calls

  • Address normalization. "5432 S Kimbark", "5432 South Kimbark Ave" and "5432 s. kimbark ave." all need to hit the same record. I wrote rules for the variants people actually paste, and tested them against real portal data.
  • Severity without a black box. A rules-based rating (elevator, heat, and structural issues outrank cosmetic ones) that users can read and argue with, instead of a model they can't.
  • Explaining the data's quirks. "No entry" inspections, where the inspector couldn't get in, show up constantly and look alarming. The tool explains what they mean rather than counting them as violations.
  • Boring technology. Workers, static assets, edge caching, no framework. The constraint was speed and clarity for a stressed person, not an impressive stack.

What's next

The data already covers the whole city; only the design is Hyde Park-specific. The next step is the other university neighborhoods in Chicago, then the city as a whole, keeping the same constraint: fast, clear, and useful to someone who has ten minutes and a lot riding on the answer.
